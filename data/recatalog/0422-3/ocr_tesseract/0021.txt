Poland’s Jews were obviously a key element in the debate over the nation’s identity.
Though somewhat over ten-percent of the population, considerably fewer than the five million
Ukrainians, who were concentrated on Poland’s eastern borders, Jews were concentrated in
cities and significantly over-represented in commerce, industry, and the professions. As early
as 1912, after the loss of an election to the Russian parliament, Dmowski’s National Democrats
made the boycott of Jewish business part of its program for "nationalizing" the Polish economy,
a process they frequently described as an "emancipation." The nationalist press, and with them
Poland’s influential Catholic daily, the Maty Dziennik, regularly complained about the
disproportionate Jewish presence in the professions and commerce. But calling for Polish
"emancipation" was to do more than advocate correction of real economic disparities. It was an
identification of Jews with the imperialist powers that had partitioned Poland. Even more
bluntly, some writers in the Catholic press described Jews as constituting a "fourth partition,"
a striking image that concealed the fact most Jews shared in the economic plight of the Poles or

were only slightly better off.
