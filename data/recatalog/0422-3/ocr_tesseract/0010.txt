Rabbi Isserles was not alone in his identification of Poland with refuge. His
contemporary, Rabbi Solomon Luria, praised the Polish kings "who many times are kind to Jews
and postpone for them the time of tax payment." Hayim ben Bezalel (1530-1588), who had
studied in Poland but then served as a rabbi in Germany, wrote: "It is known that, thank God,
His people is in this land [Poland] not despised and despoiled. Therefore a non-Jew coming
to the Jewish street has respect for the public and is afraid to behave like a villain against Jews,
while in Germany every Jew is wronged and oppressed the day long."? In the words of a
contemporary Jewish scholar from Holland, 16th century Poland had become a "second
Palestine."* The security described by the rabbis above was relative, of course, to the rest of
Europe. Jews as well as Protestants were often the objects of harassment by unruly Catholic
college students. And in previous centuries there had been riots, in Poznafi (1390) and Kraköw
(1407), instigated principally by German guilds. In the development of crafts and trade, Jews
were competitors to the Germans in Poland. To the kings and nobles, they were a valuable

resource.

were arguably the most humanitarian regulation of their status anywhere in late medieval
Europe, a condition certainly far superior to the masses of Poles who were enthralled in
