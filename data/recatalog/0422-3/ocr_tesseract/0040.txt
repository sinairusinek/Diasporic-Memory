Obviously xenophobia like antisemitism can be based on racist as well as religious motives. But
religious differences invariably heighten racial and ethic hatreds and divisions, especially when
they are countenanced by governmental and moral leadership. Condemnations of violence
voiced by religious leaders too frequently become moderated when it is "outsiders" who are the

object of that violence.

The questions raised here obtrude not ‘only upon traditional societies but Western
democracies as well with respect to issues like abortion, women’s rights, and the civil rights of
sexual minorities. The leaders and expositors of religious traditions have important contributions
to make to contemporary moral discourse about these issues. The public forum needs to hear

of the experience and wisdom contained in their respective histories and traditions. But making

one religious tradition normative for the culture is something else altogether. Giving the
leadership of the religious majority a privileged voice over others is to invite discrimination
against those others, to structure them metaphorically into "outsiders" and "guests."

It was passivity born of indifference to the plight of "outsiders" that made the Holocaust
possible. Rhetoric on behalf of national or Christian cultures helped create the conviction that
Jews were "guests" who had outstayed their welcome, a "question" requiring a solution. When
the Nazis began to advance their own "final solution" to the Jewish question, it was too late.
Even at those all-too-rare occasions when Christian voices were raised in protest and
condemnation, the metaphor was already there in place. The Catholic church, and Christian
