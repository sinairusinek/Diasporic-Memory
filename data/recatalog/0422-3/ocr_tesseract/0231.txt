What is, then, the crime of Sodom? The crime in question is
pure, unadulterated xenophobia. (Notice that the story makes clear
that the motivation is not greed, or lust). Sodom is driven by deep
hatred and fear of strangers, of that which they do not know. In
that sense the punishement of destruction reenacts the their own
desire to destroy. In psychological terms, destruction brings forth
self-destruction. To recognize the other would amount to an
acknowledgment of the self in connection with humanity, a
recognition Sodom cannot face, Hence the blindness with which Sodom
is smitten is, metaphorically, the denial of one’s humanity. To
face the strangers would be to see their own reflection in the eyes
of the strangers, and thus to recognize their own humanity and
therefore responsibility toward their fellow human beings.

Abraham’s righteousness indicates his acknowledgment of his
humanity and responsibility. His desire to save the wicked asserts
the power of righteousness. Righteousness denotes responsibility
for the stranger as well as for the hater of the stranger. The
inability of the righteous to save the wicked from themselves thus
pronounces the victory of evil and demonstrates the impotence of
righteousness.

The story presents two attempts to overcome evil by saving the
wicked. Lot sacrifices his possessions, his family, and his life to
save the wicked. (In a sense, his offering of his daughters as the
end of the family line presages the binding of Isaac). Abraham
risks his destiny as a patriarch, a destiny which has been just
reconfirmed in the promise of a son. The rhetoric of the story
suggests, however, that not to have taken the risk would have
amounted to Abraham’s failing his responsibility as the Father of
Nations. It is God who sets the test by sharing his plans with
Abraham, evidently expecting a response. The fact the it is God who
finishes the court scene indicates that he is satisfied with
Abraham’s response. In that sense, Abraham anticipates Jesus’
teaching of loving the enemies. It is not difficult to love those
who love you, says Jesus in Matthew, what counts is love for those
who hate you. Abraham’s willingness to take the risk of displeasing
God and of losing everything for the sake of Sodom exemplifies
Jesus’ teaching. Furthermore, his example is emulated in Paul’s
assertion of God’s lasting friendship for Israel because of the
patriarchs. Here it is reconciliation and love the Paul expresses
even towards those who did not accept Jesus’ message. As the father
of the Christian nations, Paul shapes his patriarchy after
Abraham’s model.

The biblical story asserts its viability through its
resistance to connect with the tragedy of the Holocaust. While
refusing association with the apocalypse, the story communicates a
meaningful lesson to the post-Holocaust world. In view of the
consciousness of the possibility of destruction, the biblical story
commands us to explore the difficult lesson in reaffirmation of
righteousness through the ethics of responsibility.
