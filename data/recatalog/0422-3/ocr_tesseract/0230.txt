Rachel Feldhay Brenner

Re-reading the story of Sodom and Gomorrah in the aftermath of the
Holocaust

The notion of the biblical story engenders the Midrashic
tradition of a biblical response to historical reality. The
consciousness of the Holocaust projects the end of this tradition
and therefore the end of the relevance of the biblical heritage.
This paper traces the ways in which the biblical story steers us
away from the nihilistic perception of ending towards a
reconstruction of the ethics of interpersonal responsibility.

Seemingly, the story of Sodom establishes correspondences with
the the Holocaust: destruction of populations, the enormity of the
destruction, the distanced God. The subsequent references to the
story in both Testaments (Deutoronomy and Isaiah; Matthew and Luke)
relate the destruction of Israel.

The conventional interpretation places the apocalyptic ending
in the context of eschatology. In view of the Holocaust apocalypse,
however, this interpretation is no longer valid. The unthinkable
convergence of eschatology and history became a reality. This
reality spells the end of the biblical story and its midrashic
interpretation. To re-view the story of Sodom as a pre-view of the
Holocaust ends the midrashic tradition and fossilizes the biblical
text.

The biblical canon, however, insists upon its midrashic
survival. The contrastive use of the Sodom imagery in the prophecy
of consolation countervails the apocalyptic ending. In both
Testaments (Ezekiel and Hosea; Matthew and Paul’s Letter to the
Romans) the story transforms condemnation and rejection into
acceptance and forgiveness. The hesitation of the text offers the
alternative of the relative perspective.

The relative perspective is offered in the story itself, in
Abraham’s suggestion of the altenative to the unalterable.
Abraham’s response to God’s judgment signals the possibility to
assert difference even in the presence of the divine, a possibility
which counteracts the notion of finality.

The question that arises: in what way is Abraham’s position
vis-a-vis God meaningful to us today? In what way does it validate
Paul’s notion of God’s eternal friendship with Israel for the sake
of the promise? To answer these questions we must understand the
concept of justice as presented by Abraham. Abraham substitutes the
convention of individual-oriented justice with a concept of
collective justice - the wicked will be saved for the sake of the
righteous. What he proposes, in fact, is that the righteous should
be capable of saving those who wish to destroy righteousness: the
salvation of the wicked will demonstrate the victory of the good
over evil.

The rhetorical arrangement of the literary materials
highlights the seriousness of the argument. It is presented in the
dramatic form of a dialogue with the divine, and the repetion of
the question intensifies the tension. Moreover, it is presented as
a court case.
