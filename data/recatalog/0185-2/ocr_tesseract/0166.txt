This is an age when everyone searches for his or her roots.
Some of it had its beginnings when the popular book "Roots" was on
every book shelf, some perhaps because we are now studying
sophisticated theories about genetics etc. and some of us are
simply searching for "connections".

Some families feel they have more relatives than they care to
deal with, and some, like myself, felt terribly alone following the
destruction of most of my relatives in the Nazi Holocaust.

A strange phenomena occurred in my search for roots. When
World War II ended, I had been in touch with two of my first
cousins whom I knew had settled in Israel, Rosa Blum and Alice
Heiser. They were the children of my mother's brother Emil who was
killed in World War I. I knew of no survivors on my father's side
of the family and thought of myself as the sole survivor.

This was difficult to comprehend as my mother was one of
eleven children and my father was one of three brothers.

In the back of my mind I recalled that my cousin Rosel
Birnbrey (Rachel Sharon) went to a Hachshara, a preparatory program
for immigration to Palestine (Israel). Following the end of World
War II and over a period of two years I placed ads on Kol Yisrael,
the Israel radio network, which had a program to help people
reunite with relatives. After the third such announcement, someone
who knew Rachel heard the announcement, contacted her, and she sent
me a telegram advising me that she was very much alive, and the
rest is history.

Now the story completely turns around. My cousins Rosa and
Alice were childless, and I am the only survivor on my mother's
side of the family, and through Rachel, who shared my quest for
roots, we discovered that there are many survivors on my father's
side of the family. There are two slight caveats to this, of
course my children and grandchildren are "survivors" of both sides
of the family, but for this epitaph I was only counting up to my
generation. The other caveat is that I did find one of my mother's
brothers after World War II, this was her half brother Albert who
was interned by the Vichy French and survived, lived and died in
France, but who also was childless.

Our Torah begins many of its themes by recalling the past and
particular by restating the names of the very people who were part
of this past. Everyone needs to know that he or she has a history,
that he or she has a story to tell, and in particular remind
ourselves and the world of the dark part of our history between
1933 and 1945, the years of Hitler.
