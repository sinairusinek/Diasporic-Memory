The Second Vatican Council in its decree on non-Christian religions (Nostra Aetate)
initiated a sea change in the way Roman Catholics and subsequently many other Christian
churches view Jews and Judaism. The council rejected the traditional albeit unofficial tradition
of blaming Jews for the death of Jesus. It repudiated the view that Jews were rejected by God
and superseded by the church. In other words, it implicitly disavowed the metaphor of the Jew
as Cain. But, as I hope to have demonstrated in the foregoing, the rationale for Catholic
antagonism against Jews in the interwar period was much more political and this-worldly than
Augustinian. For that reason, the Council’s declaration on religious liberty (Dignitatis
Humanae) is just as important as Nostra Aetate and constituted a just as much a revolution in
Catholic attitudes toward "outsiders." The official Catholic teaching prior to the Council was
that governments were not justified in treating all religions as equal. Only one faith could be
true, and error had no rights. Breaking with that profoundly illiberal stance, the Council taught
that neither truth nor error but only persons have rights -- rights which they enjoy whether they

are in error or not.

There are inferences which the Vatican, along with most other religious leaders, have
yet to draw from this break with the past. The inalienable rights of persons, even when in error,
bear implications for defining not only the nature of just governments but also the appropriate
role of the church or of any other religious body in modern pluralist societies. Governments
committed to separation of church and state are not the same as governments committed to
