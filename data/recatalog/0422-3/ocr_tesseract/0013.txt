From 1648 to 1717, Poland was embroiled in an almost uninterrupted series of wars,
beginning with the Chmielnicki uprising of cossacks and Ukrainian peasants. The Jewish
chroniclers of the 1660s and those of a century later clearly identified themselves with Poland
and the Polish cause.? Contrary to traditional Catholic practice, Jews who had embraced
Christianity to save their lives were allowed by the Polish king to revert to their practice of
Judaism. Jews also appear to have rescued a considerable amount of their worldly possessions
entrusted to non-Jewish neighbors and friends. 10 Jewish resources were obviously strained,
however, and records tell of individual Jews and Jewish communities borrowing money not only

from individual Christians but from churches and monasteries.

Contrary to the situation elsewhere in Europe, Christians in Poland, including higher
ecclesiastics and monasteries, were often the creditors of Jews, especially after the Chmielnicki
uprising. Church dignitaries were often nobles who owned their own private estates. Like other
nobles they would lease lands to Jews, rent them houses, and sell them produce. Monasteries
since the 17th century had also leased lands or lent money to Jews as long-term investments that
would earn annual returns. Polish bishops and monasteries were interested in the security of
their Jewish tenants and lease-holders. Court documents report cases in which clergy-
landowners appeared in court on behalf of "our" Jews, as they put it, and sought to mitigate
