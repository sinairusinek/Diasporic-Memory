But much to the chagrin of Catholic nationalists like those at the Maty Dziennik, ordinary
Poles by and large did not adhere to the boycott. One reads of various segments of Polish
society, from farmers to white-collar workers, preferring to do business with Jews, whether for
the sake of cheaper prices, the traditional way of doing things, credit, or courtesy. With a
frustration bordering on desperation, the Maty Dziennik regularly bemoaned the Catholic Poles’

lack of solidarity, a virtue for which it envied as well as exaggerated in the Jewish community.

To. fault Poland’s Catholic clergy and press for not being ahead of their time is hardly
fair; the Vatican had not yet entered into the era of ecumenical dialogue and did not allow any
other Catholics to do so either. My point here, however, is to demonstrate that economics alone
were not the issue. One hardly needed extraordinary vision to recognize that Poles required
precisely the kinds of skills that they could learn from Jews. Learning from Jews was not
something Catholic traditionalists could welcome, however. For Christian Poles to serve as
apprentices to Jews could only mean their being affected by Jewish thinking, and that, to

Catholic leadership at least, was unacceptable.26
