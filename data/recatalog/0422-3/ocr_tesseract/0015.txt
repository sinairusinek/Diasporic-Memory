Jews as a "Question"

The idea that Jews were a "question" was a commonplace in interwar Europe for people
of all stripes: conservative, liberal, Catholic, non-Catholic, even Jews, most notably the Zionists.
The "Polish Question" had been resolved with the defeat of the partitioning powers in World
War One, and in 1929 Italy and the Vatican came to terms on the "Roman question." But the
"Jewish question" remained intractable as ever, sometimes articulated as a problem or, as in the
Roman Jesuit journal, La Civilt Cattolica, a "danger."!* However worded and whatever its
meaning, Catholic Poles were certain that, if a "Jewish question" existed anywhere, it was
Poland. If Jews could be a "question" in Germany, where they were barely one-percent of the

population, what else could they be in Warsaw, where they made up thirty-percent of the city?

The reason why and how Jews constituted a "question" for Europe was usually ill-
defined, susceptible of a variety of socio-economic, religious or political answers. For leaders
in the Catholic church, at least, greater clarity obtained as to its origins. Though secular
historians link the "Jewish question" to 1789, the French Revolution, and the emancipation of
Jewry in France, traditionalist Catholics tended to look further back -- to 1717, to the founding
of the Grand Lodge in London and the organization of modern Freemasonry. The idea of a
Masonic-Jewish alliance has been largely forgotten today, neglected even by writers on
