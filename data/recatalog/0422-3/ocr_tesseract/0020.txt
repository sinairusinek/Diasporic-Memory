During the partitions there were Polish nationalists who repudiated their earlier
Catholicism because of the church’s conservative political stance.2? Many of them, especially
the liberal intelligentsia, entered into the ranks of Polish Freemasons. But in the heavily
Orthodox and Uniate eastern provinces, Roman Catholicism had long been known as "the Polish
religion." And though Polish nationalists had reason to feel misunderstood and betrayed by the
Vatican, schism from Rome was out of the question. In the absence of a Polish state, they
needed to draw upon Roman Catholicism as well as the Polish language and history as sources
of national identity.

Poland certainly fit Ernest Renan’s description of a nation as "a community united by
a common error with regard to its origins and a common aversion with regard to its neighbors."
Long united by an aversion to their imperialist neighbors, Poles increasing succumbed to the
erroneous identification of Poland’s origins with the advent on their soil of the Roman Catholic
church. Even though Catholicism had never been a touchstone of Polish national identity, by
the late 19th century mounting numbers of Polish clerics and popular demagogues were happy
to make it so. In an oft-quoted phrase in the nationalist and Catholic press, "Poland would be
Catholic or it would not be Poland."
