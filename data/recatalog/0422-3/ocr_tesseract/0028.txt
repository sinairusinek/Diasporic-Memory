The word "Judaization" rings as clumsy and foreign in the English language, but as early
as 1869 it was used in France to describe the changes taking place in European society. That
same year Richard Wagner wrote of "Verjüdung" in modern art. The neologism passed into
Polish as "Zazydzenie," a word which came to describe not only Poland’s urban population and
economy but the "threat" Jews posed to its historic Roman Catholic culture. The "threat" came
not only from Jews ii regarded themselves as Polish and had entered enthusiastically into
Poland’s cultural and intellectual life. It came from any Poles, whether Freemasons or not, who
attempted to advance liberal ideas and attitudes. For traditionalists bent on preserving the
identification of Polish culture with Catholicism, "Judaization" became another word for

secularity, long identified by the popes as the root of all the ills in modern society.

Epitomizing these attitudes was Janusz Rawicz, writing in a popular Catholic weekly,
Przeglad Katolicki (the Catholic Review). Rawicz confessed his sympathy for the poverty of
the Jewish masses in Poland’s small towns, and he denounced incidents of anti-Jewish violence
perpetrated by young nationalist ruffians. But he could not accede to a Jewish "falsification"
of Polish culture. Poles, he argued, were a Christian people who for a thousand years had
drunk from the wells of western culture; they wanted the kind of Poland their ancestors had
handed down to them. Rawicz granted that there were Jews in the 19th century who had
