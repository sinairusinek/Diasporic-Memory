On the Occasion of the 55th
Anniversary of Kristallnacht

יד עזרא

November 9, 1993, the 55th Anniversary of Kristallnacht. | received a bulletin from the U.S.
Holocaust Center informing me that a lecture was to take place on "Kristallnacht-Night of the Broken
Glass". The speaker was to be a C. Brooks Peters, a New York Times correspondent who "scooped"
the world when he reported on the happenings of that infamous night and who was the sole reporter
to witness this event.

Statistics show that 90 people were killed that night (The Encyclopedia Judaica lists 36 Jews killed),
although 90 people is a big number to those who had loved ones among the 90 people, it seems
minuscule when compared to the number of martyrs of the entire holocaust.

My father was one of those unfortunate ones, yet I am not even certain if he was considered one of
the ninety, as he was severely injured on that night, suffered from the wounds sustained that night,
and died almost three months later.

Just like we always talk about the many sad things which happened in Jewish history on Tisha B'Av,
November 9 has a unique place in German history: In 1918 the German Kaiser fled Germany prior
to the surrender and end of World War I; In 1923 Hitler's Putsch was aborted in Munich; in 1938
it was Kristallnacht and during recent history it was the day on which the Berlin Wall came down.
It almost appears that some dates are destined for history.

It is difficult to assess if Hitler needed an excuse to begin his attack on the Jews or if events simply
brought this about.

It is wrong to try to rationalize or justify why atrocities occurred, yet many historical events generally
are the result of a sequence of events.

In 1917 the German industrial complex found itself short of manpower on account of the war, and
an anti-semitic German general (who later became a part of the Nazi regime) by the name of Erich
Ludendorff, put up posters in Poland and in the Pale of Russia, in Yiddish, with the headline "Meine
lieben Yidden" (my dear Jews) and invited them to come to Germany to work for decent wages in the
steel mills and coal mines. This was welcome news to many Jews,as most of them had lived in
poverty in the Shtetls of Poland. Many families responded and came to Germany, and in particular
to the Ruhr Basin where we lived.
