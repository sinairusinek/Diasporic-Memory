When antisemitic college students engaged in smashing store windows or assaulting
Jewish students, the usual response in the interwar Catholic press was first to criticize the
violence but then explain it in such a way as to excuse it.45 Father Szymafiski, on the contrary,
expressed genuine outrage. It did not matter that only a handful of students were responsible
for these outbreaks or that the police had put them down. Student thoughtlessness was not the
major cause of the violence but the "perversity and bad will" of their ringleaders. In response
to those who claimed that these were reprisals for Jewish transgressions, he answered that
Christian ethics did not permit revenge by vigilantes. Christ the Good Shepherd promised to

care for sheep, not wolves.*6

Father Szymafiski was openly sympathetic to the Jewish predicament, but he was no
liberal. The popes had made liberal and Catholic antithetic concepts. Szymaziski, like the
Vatican and Polish bishops, favored a Catholic Poland. He contended that a religious state was
more tolerant than one confessionally neutral. He rejected the arguments for civil marriage and
for religiously neutral schools; he regarded a secular world view as essentially atheistic. It was
reasonable, he argued, for Poles to want to preserve a Christian culture. But this meant more
than preferring Poles for positions of leadership in academia, the professions, and government.
It meant preferring Poles with Christian convictions, since there were Polish intellectuals

endeavoring to make Polish culture secular.
