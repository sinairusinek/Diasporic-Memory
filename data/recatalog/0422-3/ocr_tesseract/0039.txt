Living in a pluralist society has become dangerously problematic as once again -- and not
only in Germany or Europe -- one hears the rhetoric of "host" and "guest," or worse, such
martial metaphors as "fortress," "foreign invasion," and "self-defense." Hatred and violence
against outsiders runs rampant in all parts of the globe -- in the United States, Central and
Western Europe, the Balkans, India, South Africa, and the Middle East. The reasons for the
violence are cultural as well as economic, a reaction to the danger that foreigners are said to
Pose to national identity and culture. Even in post-Christian Europe, it is not by chance that
Muslim Algerians in France and Turks in Germany are the most frequent targets of xenophobic
violence, the foreigners viewed as the greatest threat to cultural identity and ethnic purity.

How a society treats the minorities in its midst is arguably the single most telling
criterion of its morality and civilization. But can a state treat "outsiders" with fairness and
dignity if it is committed to favoring one particular religious system and/or the culture it
created? Are there limits to the rights of a majority to impose its moral code on the entire body
politic of a nation? Is an officially self-declared Christian, Jewish, or Islamic state inherently

undemocratic and anachronistic? Does it necessarily divide societies into hosts and guests?
