According to a 16th century Italian rhyme, pre-partition Poland was "heaven for the
nobility, purgatory for city-dwellers, hell for the peasants, and paradise for the Jews."!? Despite
its exaggerations, there was a kernel of truth to the saying. As much as any nation, pre-
partitioned Poland belied what the late revered Jewish historiographer, Salo Wittmayer Baron,
criticized as the "lachrymose view of Jewish history," as if the fate of Jews in diaspora was
unremittent misery and persecution. Poland was no paradise. Jews were caught between
sympathetic nobles and unsympathetic clergy, oppressive land-owners and exploited peasants.
But Jews were allowed to live more freely and, until the 1648 Ukrainian uprising and subsequent
invasions, more securely in Poland than anywhere else in Europe. Even the attitude of the

Polish church vacillated between self-interested protection of Jews and benign neglect.

With its tradition of tolerating dissidents, late medieval Poland became and long remained
what Babylonia and then Spain had been earlier, the spiritual center of world Jewry and principal
wellspring of its learning. To appreciate how it went from being a spiritual center for Jews to
being a cemetery entails not only the whole of Holocaust studies but of modern European history
altogether. What happened this century in Warsaw had its roots not only in Berlin but Paris and
Rome. The revolutions that rocked 19th century Europe rocked the Roman Catholic church as
