Another even more mischievous image commonly used in the Polish Catholic press was
that employed by the Prime Minister in a 1936 speech to the Sejm, the Polish parliament. He
repudiated violence against Jews but approved of an "economic struggle": "An honest host," he
stated, "does not allow anybody to be harmed in his house." No matter that Jews were citizens
of the Polish Republic, most of whose forebears had been in Poland for centuries. Poles were
hosts and Jews were guests. The implications of the metaphor were obvious. Guests do not

gain proprietary rights and easily outstay their welcome.

Though "nationalizing" Poland’s economy by boycotting Jewish business had been a
program of the National Democrats since 1912, its highest and most celebrated Catholic
endorsement came in 1936. Poland’s leading prelate, Cardinal August Hlond, issued a pastoral
letter on a variety of topics, among them the boycott and incidents of anti-Jewish violence incited
by young nationalists. "One may love one’s own nation more," he wrote," but one may not hate
anyone. Not even Jews. It is good to prefer your own kind when shopping, to avoid Jewish
stores and Jewish stalls in the marketplace, but it is forbidden to demolish a Jewish store,

damage their merchandise, break windows, or throw things at their homes."

Reading the Catholic periodical literature subsequent to the Cardinal’s letter, one is struck
at how rarely it was cited. The impression one receives is that the Cardinal’s views were hardly
news. In point of fact, the clergy and Catholic press, above all the Maty Dziennik, were very
much in the forefront of the effort to "nationalize" Poland’s economy. Polish church leaders
