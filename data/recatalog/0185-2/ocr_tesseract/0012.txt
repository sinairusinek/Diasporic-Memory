Stettin (Szezecin) 1994

As long as I have lived I often heard the name of Stettin mentioned. First, when I was
a child I knew it as the birth place of my father and then on account of a steady
correspondence between my father and his brother Siegfried, and later when I searched for
survivors and found Rachel Sharon and later many more relatives and of course nowadays as
I have taken this genealogy work more seriously.

Stettin was always just a place on a map, a thought on my mind and perhaps there was
a little bit of curiosity, what kind of city it was from which so many generation of Birnbreys
had hailed.

This year I had an opportunity to visit Eastern Europe and I arranged to visit this
mysterious city of Stettin. In order to maximize this experience we arranged to have a
"Jewish Guide" meet us and show us the city.

First, let me put the city in some historic perspective. Stettin, which is now called
Szczecin, has been under more flags than "Six Flags over Georgia". It had been German for
the 500 years preceding the end of World War II, and, at different times, a part of Denmark,
Poland, Russia, Prussia, Sweden, Magdeburg plus some smaller Dukedoms or Kingdoms.

Jews were first mentioned in Stettin in the year 1261, and, according to sources, the Jews
of Stettin were the first Jews deported in Germany during the Nazi period. The Jewish
population numbered 2,365 in 1933.

The city itself had a population of close to 500,000 at one time and was important as a
Baltic seaport and center for ship building industry.

At the end of World War II the population had dwindled to 8,000 inhabitants and when
the city and the area were deeded to Poland under the Oder-Neisse agreement, the entire
German population abandoned the city and a Polish population took its place, which included
changing all of the street names to Polish names and Polish became the official language of
the city. In other words virtually a 100% population shift.

Likewise, the Jewish population became an entire new group of Jews, all of them
originating from Poland and Russia.
