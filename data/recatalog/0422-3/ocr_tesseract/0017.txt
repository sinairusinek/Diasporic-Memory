At the beginning of this century, Pope Leo XIII described Freemasonry as "the
permanent personification of revolution."!® By that time Italian Freemasons had been
significantly involved in the successful effort to reunify Italy and transform it into a modern
secular state. In the most detailed pronouncement on it before or since, Pope Leo XIII
denounced Freemasonry in 1884 for what he called the "great error of this age," namely,
treating the Catholic church as equal to other religions. He charged Freemasons with
responsibility for depriving the papacy of its temporal sovereignty and for introducing secular
education, civil marriage and divorce into Italy. In 1892, Pope Leo advised Italian Catholics
to avoid familiarity or friendship not only with known or suspected Masons but even with "those
who hide under the mask of universal tolerance, respect for all religions, and the craving to
reconcile the maxims of the Gospel with those of the revolution."!9

First in France then Italy, Freemasons had become identified with liberalism and
revolution. It was in Germany that they first became identified with Jews. The first known
instance of a Jew being admitted to a Masonic lodge was in London in 1732. There is no reason
to believe that the authors of the English Masonic constitutions intended their toleration of
Christian diversity to allow for Jewish membership, but when Jewish candidates applied, access
to the lodge was extended to them as well. Not so in Germany, however, where traditionalist
forces bent on preserving the old social order prevailed well into the late 19th century. So it
