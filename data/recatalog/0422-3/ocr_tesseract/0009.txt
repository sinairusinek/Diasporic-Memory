Many if not most Jews today tend to define Poland as endemically antisemitic and, since
the Holocaust, as little more than a cemetery.”? Conflicting metaphors have long been
problematic for Polish Jewish relations, particularly in this century when, in the interwar period,
Poland too with the rest of Europe was swept up by the wave of antisemitism that emanated
from Germany. The Catholic church and its press strongly rejected the murderous racist
antisemitism of the Nazis. But its own metaphors made for a rhetoric that virtually guaranteed
indifference to the Jewish plight or at least rendered Catholic sympathy for it difficult. Interwar
Poland was home to the largest aggregate of Jews in Europe, somewhat over three-mil
lion, but two-thirds of its population was Roman Catholic. That made the attitudes of the
Catholic press toward Jews particularly significant. Those attitudes were encapsulated in
metaphors warranting critical analysis and reflection here. Though not traditional and of
foreign, particularly French provenance, they have a resilience that makes them enduring,

universal, and reusable for our own time.
