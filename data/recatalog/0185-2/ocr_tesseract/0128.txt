It appears that my grandmother was the daughter of Marcus Hirsch Levy and Ernestinn
Marcus Friedlander Levy and that they lived in a town named Lämmersdorf.

My father had two brothers, though until I met Rachel Sharon, I was aware of only one. One
brother was Siegfried Birnbrey who was Rachel's father and who was married to Elisabet
Bitterman (whose family name appears throughout the family tree). It is my understanding
that he had some type of textile factory next door to the railroad station and when one exited
the station, the name "Birnbrey" was prominently visible. My father and his brother Siegfried
kept in touch through a very frequent exchange of letters but otherwise, due to the distance,
saw very little of one another.

When I met Rachel in Berlin she let me in on a family secret. One day when she came
home from school, she related to her parents that she and the other children had made fun
of a crippled man on the way home. In describing the incident and the person, her parents
explained to her that this person was her uncle, a brother of her father (and my father).
About a year after my meeting with Rachel we were notified of his death and asked to
contribute funds to pay for his funeral. These two incidents were my only knowledge of this
third Birnbrey brother until August 1994, when I met his daughter-in-law, Zilla Birnbrei in
Berlin.

A year before I left for the U.S. we were called to the Police Station in Dortmund. It
appeared that we had inherited some money from a relative in San Francisco, we had to sign
some papers and the funds were promptly confiscated by the Germans. This is one
connection I never was able to figure out, though my father did speak about San Francisco
relatives. Rachel told me that one of my grandfather's sisters married a man named Bernstein
who lived in San Francisco and that she tried to contact him but that he was very rude to her.

My father moved from Stettin to Stuttgart where he was employed by an insurance company.
When World War I broke out he served in the Wiirttemberg Army, was wounded, earned the
Iron Cross and somehow met my mother who was then a widow. He spent his entire life
keeping up with many army buddies both by correspondence and frequent visits. None of
these buddies were Jewish and most of them continued some type of relationship with him
during the Hitler period.

The largest part of this family tree is attributed to Alfons Gerson who did a fantastic job in
putting together the Gerson family tree and whose daughter Erika coincidentally now lives
in Dortmund.

One of my uncle Siegfried's daughters died of pneumonia at age 14 while I still lived in

Dortmund, one daughter, Rachel, made Aliyah to Israel and the rest of the family appeared
to have been victims of the Holocaust.
