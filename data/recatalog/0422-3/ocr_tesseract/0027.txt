This time the monastery of Conventual Franciscans, founded by Kolbe in 1927, took up the
Pope’s challenge. In 1935 it began publishing the Maty_Dziennik (Little Daily). Kolbe himself
was laboring in Japan at the time, but the following year he returned to Poland to become the
Superior of the monastery and its ambitious publishing industry. He had general indirect
supervision over the Maty Dziennik and saw its circulation increase from a 100,000 copies an

issue in 1935 to 300,000 by the end of 1938, more than any other daily in Poland.

Kolbe’s was a militant Catholicism. Convinced that the forces that secularized France
and Italy were at work in Poland, he set out to oppose them aggressively. Kolbe himself wrote
little for the publications he supervised and even less about Jews. On one occasion he advised
in a letter against stirring up animosity against Freemasons or Jews; love was the only way to
convert them. On the issue of the boycott, Kolbe suggested that it was better not to speak of
excluding Jews but rather of developing Polish enterprises. The Maty Dziennik, however, rarely

made such distinctions.

If antisemitism is defined as hostility against Jews as Jews, Maximilian Kolbe was not
antisemitic. He did not exhibit animosity toward religious or traditional Jews. But quite in
keeping with the policies of the Holy See and Catholic Action, he was anti-liberal, anti-
Masonic, and anti-secular. He was hostile to any proponent of modern, secular culture, Jew or
Gentile. He believed that modern secularity, Polish or Jewish, must be resisted militantly as a
danger both to the church and to Poland. His attitude toward Jews was very much like that of

Pope Pius XI and the Catholic orthodoxy of his day: anti-racist but conversionary, anti-Nazi
