Joseph Walk — the alte Heimat reversal

A Breslau-born historian on returning to Germany without Heimweh — Jerusalem, April 1991

From Anne Betten's Emigrantendeutsch in Israel corpus (Israelkorpus IS, 186 speakers, recorded 1989–94). Prof. Joseph Walk (1914–2005), educator and historian, long-time director of the Leo Baeck Institute Jerusalem. Eight segments of a 133-minute interview, rendered here in paraphrase; timecodes link each turn to the aligned audio held at the Datenbank für Gesprochenes Deutsch, Mannheim.

Summary

The corpus's deepest reflection on the Heimat question — delivered by a speaker who denies having any. Walk, a conscious Zionist raised (as he tells it) under a portrait of Theodor Herzl, first returned to Germany in 1967 and by 1991 counts many German houses where he can arrive and stay at any time — yet his first re-encounter with Breslau leaves him cold, and when a Hamburg official asks how hard it was to adjust to a new Heimat, he answers that for him Israel had been the old one all along.

Around this reversal the interview stages the whole Heimat-axis repertoire: symposium invitations, the 1932 Deutschtum taunt and its bitter 1942 echo in the moshav, the telling book title Neue Heimat, his wife's first border crossing, and a second-generation colleague who swore never to go — and went.

    Segment 1 · The invitation 00:01:17 · c6 an invitation to a German symposium, received days before the interview

    Walk Walk opens with something that happened only days earlier, because it belongs, he says, directly to the interview's theme: an invitation had arrived to a symposium in Germany on ethics and politics — he would be the only Israeli. On the phone, the organizer proposed a lecture title built on the word Anspruch, which Walk spontaneously heard as the Jewish claim to the Land — a theological matter, he objected, more than an ethical one — and the conversation broke off. The next morning it struck him that he might have misread the word; the Duden confirmed that it can equally mean the demand one places on oneself, which was of course what had been meant. For him, a textbook demonstration — from that very week — of how far his feel for German had fallen behind.

    Segment 2 · First return, 1967 00:05:40 · c6 the school secretary, and a compliment in Mülheim

    Walk His first return to Germany after emigrating came in 1967. Waiting for a headmaster in a school office, he heard the secretary use two words he took for errors — and told the headmaster, apologetically, that the woman's German was faulty. The headmaster turned the correction around: it was Walk's German, he said, that had stayed behind, and explained the current usage. That, Walk says, was his first encounter with spoken contemporary German.

    Walk A second episode, half a year before the interview: at a symposium in Mülheim on education for peace — again the only Israeli — a German woman in her early twenties approached him the morning after his lecture to tell him that he embodied for her the culture of the Weimar Republic. A startling compliment for a German Jew, he says, and one that left him thinking: perhaps the German Jews really are the only heirs of Weimar culture.

    Segment 3 · The Deutschtum taunt 00:26:50 · c32 1932 in Köln — and its 1942 echo in the moshav

    Walk He prefaces the story with what he calls its essential background: a thoroughly Zionist upbringing — born, as he puts it, under a portrait of Theodor Herzl — and a 1932 Abitur essay, written in a German school, that closed with the hope of one day helping to educate a new generation in Palestine. That summer, at the Jewish teachers' seminary in Köln, a school inspector went down the row asking the students their plans. When Walk, second in line, answered that he would go to Palestine, his own teacher — the seminary being orthodox and decidedly German in orientation — permitted himself the taunt that Walk would end up over there as a representative of Deutschtum abroad. The students smiled; so did the well-meaning inspector.

    Walk He reached Palestine in 1936, led a Youth Aliyah group, and stayed on as teacher in a moshav where Jeckes were a resented quarter of the population. Around 1942, as news of the destruction seeped in, the party paper ran attacks that slid from German culture to the Jeckes themselves — capped, at the village assembly, by the assertion that Goethe had been Hitler's teacher. A friend's article in the culture committee's spoken newspaper defending the distinction between a people, its culture and its regime was banned by majority vote; Walk smuggled it through as a letter to the editor, and at the annual assembly found himself accused — by neighbours, now, not Nazis — of defending German culture while the dead lay before them. Confronted a second time with the charge of representing Deutschtum abroad, he resigned from the culture committee and never rejoined it — the worst experience, he says, of his life as a Jecke in the country.

    Segment 4 · Neue Heimat 00:38:57 · c48–c52 a book title as the whole German-Jewish predicament

    Walk The Leo-Baeck-Institut, he relates, had at last brought out a Hebrew volume — parallel to the London and New York institutes — on the German Jews' integration and their contribution to building the country; its title, he notes, is the telling one: Neue Heimat — "New Homeland".

    BettenBetten asks whether that is Gelber's book.

    WalkIt is.

    Walk He had opened his own lecture on the volume, he says, by asking what Polish Jew would ever have given a book that name. For him the title holds the entire difficulty of the German Jews, who in their great majority had genuinely felt at home in Germany — himself, he concedes, to a degree included: culturally certainly, and up to a certain point politically as well.

    Segment 5 · The border crossing 00:45:08 · c62 his wife's first trip back — her parents did not survive

    Walk He remembers precisely, he says, his wife's first journey back to Germany — her parents had been murdered — and the moment they crossed the border from Switzerland: her first re-encounter with the sharp German official tone, at which they both flinched, though he had already been over several times and finds the tone much softened today (it survived longer, he adds, in East Berlin). He closes the parenthesis on a lighter note: for earlier trips he would pack a necktie for Germany — a thing he would never wear at home — and it pleases him that in recent years the necktie, too, has been disappearing over there.

    Segment 6 · The colleague who swore never to go 01:10:42 · c94 second-generation refusal — and what happened when he went

    Walk Some years earlier a colleague fifteen or twenty years his junior sought him out, bewildered by his own experience: he had sworn never to set foot in Germany — the second generation, Walk observes, can be no less absolute than the first — had finally gone once, and came back to report that he had found more of a shared language, in both senses of the word, with German colleagues than with many colleagues at home. Walk explained it to him as the common cultural inheritance at work in an open-minded new generation — and admits the experience mirrors his own: he has real friends in Germany, and counts many houses there where he is at home in the plain sense that he can arrive at any time, stay, and feel well.

    Segment 7 · Breslau, without Heimatgefühl 01:26:30 · c137–c148 the first re-encounter with the hometown — and the reversal of the "old Heimat"

    Betten Betten recalls attending, the previous year, Walk's lecture on his first re-encounter with Breslau, and puts to him the question his publications and interviews keep circling — where he feels at home, whether the German language is still a home — and asks directly: does he still have Heimat feelings for Breslau?

    WalkNone whatever, he answers — none at all. The visit itself made that plain to him: he found he had no connection to the city, and was surprised to discover how little of its history he knew — his Polish colleague was far better versed — and how little any of it touched him. What did hold him was Breslau's great Jewish community.

    Walk In that large, close community, he explains, he had found everything he needed. The small-town Jews of Baden-Württemberg in the west were another world — he came to know it editing the region's Gemeindebuch — and their attachment to place he can empathise with but never shared; in the big city, he suggests, that kind of Heimat feeling develops far more weakly.

    Betten Was it hard for him, then, she asks, to understand people of his generation for whom religion was not the centre — who could not take root in the strange new country, and were left with melancholy, perhaps even a kind of Heimweh?

    Walk Understand it he can, he says, and even feel for it. But he recounts his astonishment in Hamburg, where an official who had helped him reach certain files asked him, on a walk, whether it had not been terribly hard to settle into a new Heimat. He stopped in the street, taken aback, and answered that the difficulty was simply unknown to him — for him, the country he had gone to had been the old Heimat all along. One who left at twenty-two, he adds, cannot be compared with those uprooted at forty or fifty, with professions and standing in Germany; the official could not understand him, nor he the official.

    Segment 8 · Dr. Seidel 01:53:28 · c210 the one German teacher — and the first post-war inquiry, eight years on

    Walk Of his non-Jewish teachers only one left a mark: Dr. Seidel — Social Democrat and religious, close to Walk in outlook, in an otherwise right-leaning school. When Walk chose Heine as his 1932 Abitur topic — deliberately, so that the teachers would hear for once what a German Jew could feel in Germany — Seidel's response was a warning against making a coquetry of one's own suffering, a sentence Walk says he took with him into his life as an educator. At their last meeting, in 1934 — Seidel had been suspended, then punitively transferred to a Nazi-dominated school — the teacher explained that he would now have to join the NS teachers' association: at forty, newly married, a veteran of the first war, was he supposed to abandon his Vaterland? A Nazi, he promised, he would never be.

    Walk After the war, Walk relates, he never asked after the fate of Germans he had known — until, eight years on, Seidel became the first and only one he sought out. He traced the widow, who had moved to West Germany, and wrote to ask what had become of her husband; her reply — she marvelled that he could write such a letter at all — told him Seidel had fallen in the final defence of Breslau. Walk later visited her in Germany several times, and they kept up a correspondence — for him, he says, one more remembrance of the Weimar era: the one German teacher who remained a model.
