In the wake of the Reformation, Poland became a haven of safety for more than Jews.

Between 1550 and 1650, when religious dissenters were routinely executed in England and

Holland, Poland was notable for its lack of internecine blood-letting. The Polish nobility were
resolved to prevent any such occurrence like France’s Saint Bartholomew’s Day massacre from
occurring in Poland. The so-called (1573) pacta conventa passed by the electoral diet required
that Poland’s kings safeguard the religious freedom of all residents on Polish lands, including
Jews: "Since Turks, Armenians, Tartars, Greeks, and Jews not only sojourn in Poland but also
reside here and (freely) move from place to place, they ought (undisturbed) to press their faiths,

enjoy their liberties, and benefit, so to say, from the same rights of citizenship. „7

This lack of fanaticism among the Polish nobility was not without impact on the Polish
bishops. From time to time church synods demanded implementation of canons regarding the
segregation of Jews from Christian society, but the resolutions were not vigorously pursued.
