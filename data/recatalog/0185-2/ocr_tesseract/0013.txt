Our "Jewish" guide turned out not to be a Jewish guide. The last and only "Jewish" tour
he had given was about five years ago. But to his credit, he did some research for us and
presented us with copies of various documents, including a List of Jews in Pomerania,
published in October 1935 which included the names and addresses of my grandfather (my
mother's father) and my uncle Siegfried, (Rachel's father and my father's brother).

We arrived on a Sunday, which turned out to be bad planning on our part, as many
places were closed, including the Jewish Community Building. Besides the normal tour a
tour guide gives, I specifically asked to see particular sites which were of interest to us.

First we had to locate a pre-World War II map to locate the streets by their former
German names and discover their locations according to their current Polish names.

The house of the Birnbreys was on a street named "Schiitzengraben". The street is no
longer there, and the location of that street is simply a grassed area. However, this visit
made me understand the name of the street. Above the street is a beautiful castle, and
"Schiitzengraben" means "trenches of the guards" and possibly the guards of the castle lived
there, as an old moat is immediately next to the former street.

Ruth Kurzion gave me the address of her old home, and after converting the location to
the new Polish name, we actually found the original house. We were told that none of the
house numbers had been changed, only the names of streets.

Where the synagogue once stood is now a grassed area and an apartment house. There
is no marker to indicate that there once was a synagogue which was burned down by the
Nazis on Kristallnacht.

One of my genealogy publications mentioned the location of two former Jewish
cemeteries. The old one, in the heart of the city, had been completely destroyed by the
World War II bombing of the city, but someone made a sort of collage type monument out
of about 21 Jewish headstones as a memorial.
