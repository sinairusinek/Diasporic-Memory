Zilla could not understand the difference in the spelling of our name. Frankly, I can't either.
However in my family prayer book, my grandfather's name is spelled Birnbrey, in a directory
of the Stettin Jewish community my uncle's name is spelled Birnbrey, my birth certificate
reads Birnbrey, yet on my German passport my name is Birnbrei. Zilla made me a copy of
Hermann's wedding license and his death certificate, and on both of those documents his
name is spelled Birnbrei.

Zilla spent ten years, including the war years, in a Jewish orphans home in Prague and has
a daughter by a prior marriage. She gave me a picture of her husband Günter, and there is
a tremendous resemblance between him and my father.

The story should really end here, but, first of all we hope that this is only the beginning of
our relationship with our newly found cousin Zilla and there is more.

I had read a story about this huge Jewish cemetery in Berlin. The next day we set out to find
out more about it. It appears that the Weissensee Jewish Cemetery, located in what was
formerly East Berlin, is a cemetery containing 130,000 graves, and, which were not molested
by the Nazis. I was told that the cemetery had poor records and if I wanted to go through
a list of 130,000 names I might find something.

I called the person in charge, a Mrs. Borkman. Mrs. Borkman was extremely nice and told
me that the information I had gotten was incorrect and that they do have an index card file
arranged alphabetically. Lo and behold, I found the grave of my grandfather (my mother's
father) as well as his second wife who died on the same day. This cemetery contains some
remarkable and unusual grave stones.

While we were in Mrs. Borkman's office an argument ensued between the management and
a couple of grave diggers. This is an argument which could have taken place in any one of
our businesses or offices. The argument was about the cemetery's policy to refrain from work
on the Jewish holidays vs. having to work on the non-Jewish holidays. This argument
brought me back to reality.

The grave stone, clear of any pebbles, indicated no previous visitors. We recited the kaddish

and placed a couple of pebbles on the stone, which probably marked the first tangible sign
of a visitor in over fifty years.
