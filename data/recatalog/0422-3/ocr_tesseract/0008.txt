Linguists and philosophers alike have given considerable attention in recent years to our

use of metaphors to construct and define reality. It is by means of metaphors that we make
sense of our experiences. We cannot explain anything except in terms of other things. "In
order to understand the world and function in it, we have to categorize, in ways that made sense
to us, the things ‏ו ל‎ we encounter."! Metaphors play a particularly crucial role in
our construction of social and political realities. Communities and nations unite themselves
around symbols that both define and distinguish them from their neighbors. It was with
metaphors that Catholic Poles traditionally defined themselves vis-a-vis the rest of Europe, and
with metaphorical language that they defined their relationship to Jews.

During the dark night of Poland’s partitions, Polish nationalists, even those who were not
religious, drew upon the store of Christian symbolism to idealize Poland as the Christ among
nations, crucified and buried. The Poland that re-appeared on the map of Europe in 1918 was
commonly referred to as zmartwychwstana, "resurrected." But even after the trauma of the
partitions was over, memories of two failed insurrections against czarist Russia and of the
vigorous efforts of Prussia to stamp out Polish national consciousness continued to incline Poles
to define themselves in terms of victimhood and martyrdom. Needless to say, using messianic
imagery to understand one’s people encumbers anything like national self-criticism. And a high
