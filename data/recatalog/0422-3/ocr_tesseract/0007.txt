Abstract:

The metaphors whereby Catholic Poles understood themselves and the Jews in their midst
were not the same in the interwar period as those before the partitions. In medieval Poland Jews
were interpreted with the traditional Augustinian metaphor of Cain, as Poles defined themselves
as the bastion of Western Christendom. In "resurrected" Poland, Catholic traditionalists accepted
the metaphor of Jews constituting a "question," but regarded it as more serious in Poland than
anywhere else, given Jewish numbers and their disproportionate representation in Poland’s

economic and professional life.

The myth of a Masonic-Jewish alliance to subvert Christian society and culture first
flourished in late 19th century France but held considerable sway over Catholic thinking in
interwar Poland. Following Vatican policy, both Catholic Action and the Catholic press in
Poland assumed a militant posture against efforts by Masonic and other Polish liberals to separate
church and state and foster a secular Polish culture. The Catholic battle against liberalism, lost
in France and Italy, was continued between the wars in Poland. Assimilated Jews were defined
as "outsiders" from the East and as "guests," but particularly as symbols and agents of secularity.
Traditionalist Catholics saw themselves as engaged in a "cultural war" against the "Judaization,"
that is to say the "secularization" of Poland’s supposedly traditional Catholic culture. The
metaphors used in that war are being re-used elsewhere around the world today. Rhetoric on
behalf of religious of national cultures can only create attitudes of indifference once again to the

plight of those deemed different.
