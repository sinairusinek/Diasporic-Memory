Two Families — Wolfsohn & Schiff

A family history by Marianne Barton, née Schiff

Written in Honiton, Devon · completed 1999 · two books, c. 180 pp.

Summary

A two-book family history written in Honiton, Devon (1999) by Marianne Barton, née Schiff. Book I traces the Wolfsohn line back through her mother's father Dr. Alfred Wolfsohn — including Max Wolfsohn's takeover of Robert Koch's general practice in Wollstein. Book II covers the Schiff and Baschwitz families.

The pivot moment is the 1938 visit of the grandparents to Mandate Palestine and the grandfather's refusal to overstay: "He wanted to do things in good German order."

    Introduction Honiton, Devon · 1999

    For my son Ilan Shefer, his wife Marciene Shapiro and their sons, Brian and Randy.

    I have written as much as I know about my family's history, so that all this knowledge I have of our ancestors, either through personal reminiscences, or through documentation, will not be lost, when I shall no longer be around.

    At that time in the future, you might like to have some information of all those figures of the past, some of whose genes you might well be carrying, and who will you turn to with your questions, when I myself shall be but a memory?

    Apart from all the stories I have heard from my grandparents, from my parents, and from my cousins, I was lucky to get the Autobiography of my great-aunt Grete Sochaczewer, which was sent to me by her daughter-in-law Gerti Sawyer in Lisbon. It contains invaluable material. More help came from my cousin Haim Caro in Geva, Israel, who assiduously collected innumerable family documents, and had them all photocopied and translated them into Hebrew for the sake of his family.

    More help came from Dr. Cécile Lowenthal-Hensel, a descendant of the Mendelssohn family, and a historian who lives in Berlin. She sent material from a rare book in her possession, about Jewish Marriages in Berlin, 1759–1815.

    Chapter I · My Mother's parents — and Robert Koch Wollstein, Posen · c. 1870

    My mother's father was Dr. Alfred Wolfsohn, M.D., son of Max Meyer Wolfsohn, also a doctor, and the son of a rabbi. They lived in the small town of Wollstein in the Province of Posen in East Germany.

    Max Wolfsohn took over Dr. Robert Koch's patients when the latter gave up his practice in order to devote himself entirely to research. Dr. Koch also lived in Wollstein, and had his surgery there. However, one day his wife gave him a microscope for his birthday and that changed the course of medical history.

    In the second half of the 19th century, medical diagnosis was based on the doctor's experience and observation, and less on scientific research as it is practised nowadays. A country doctor was not too well off. Often he was paid in kind by the farmers, artisans, and workers he was treating, as these had no cash either. Therefore a microscope was a luxury; Mrs. Koch probably saved up for this birthday present for a long time […].

    As soon as Dr. Koch started to gaze through his microscope, he discovered wonderful things going on in the world unseen by the human eye. […] Among others he discovered the bacillus causing tuberculosis. As his research took more and more time, he handed his patients over to Meyer Wolfsohn, the only other doctor in town willing to do so. Therefore great-grandpa Meyer has a share in Dr. Koch's discoveries.

    […] Julius Wolfsohn also was a doctor in Berlin. I know he had a daughter called Else who married somebody called Neuberg; they had twins of whom only the son survived. They emigrated to New Zealand in 1933. Their son Dr. Klaus Neuberg lives in England; I tried to get in touch with him, but he never answered.

    Chapter II · The house in the Brillerstrasse Wuppertal · 1930s

    Back to the house in the Brillerstrasse in Wuppertal: Below the kitchen was the cellar which included a wine cellar, and what was called in Germany a Wash-kitchen. Once a month it was washing day. All the washing was done by hand. There was a huge boiler in which all the washing was boiled. The only luxury my Grandma had was a centrifuge for wringing. Otherwise all the work was done by two washerwomen.

    You could always tell in Wuppertal when washing was done in a house, because the cellars had small windows at street level, and the steam was pouring out.

    The entrance to the house was sumptuous, with an empty hall leading to double doors. […] A door led from the empty hall into my Grandfather's waiting room, where there was a huge terrestrial globe on a stand and antlers hanging from the walls. From the entrance, as well as from the waiting room, doors led into the surgery (practice room).

    Chapter III · Coming to Palestine — the certificate, the Arlosoroff circle Berlin → Jerusalem · 1937

    If a person who wished to emigrate to Palestine had 1000 Marks, he could get on a list to get a certificate of entry. My parents were as poor as churchmice.

    My father had various Zionist connections from his younger days, when he had been a member of the Jewish Labour Party together with such personalities as Chaim Arlosoroff. He tried to get their help, which took ages, and umpteen letters of refusal. All these letters I found in a huge file after my mother's death.

    Chapter IV · 1938 — the grandparents visit, and refuse to overstay Palestine · 1938

    In 1938 my Grandparents came as tourists to visit us, and to see how they could settle in Palestine, which was held as a Mandate by the British Government. They only allowed a certain "quota" of Jewish immigrants in. In order to enter legally you had to apply for a "Certificate". You also had either to be recommended and vouched for, or bring in 1000 Marks, which in those days was quite a lot of money.

    If you did not have either, you could come as a tourist, and "vanish" into a kibbutz or moshav, go into hiding for a while, and later mingle with the rest of the population. Nobody really paid attention to that.

    The whole family urged my grandparents to stay on illegally, but my grandfather would not hear of it. He was of the old school. In 1938 he was 77 years old, and set in his ways. He wanted to do things in good German order. Sell the house, and come legally with 1000 Marks.

    In vain, everyone told him that there was really no time for that. He was sure he could do it, instead of having to live on the small income of his children, that is, being dependent on them, and crowding together in a small flat.

    G-F-0042 · p. ~33
    Chapter V · The cancelled train late 1930s

    Twice a year we, that is to say, my mother and I, travelled to see her parents. Even in those days it was dangerous, after having emigrated, to return to Germany. My father was always worried when we went.

    One day we were all ready to go, when we heard of a Jewish mother and child who had been on the train from Paris to Germany, and were arrested on the border, and never heard of again. So the trip was cancelled.

    G-F-0042 · p. ~78
    Chapter VI · Toscanini at the Tel Aviv Exhibition Centre Tel Aviv · 1936

    It must have been around that time that Arturo Toscanini came to Palestine, for the opening of what is now the Israel Philharmonic Orchestra. A very basic concert hall had been built in the grounds of the Tel Aviv Exhibition Centre.

    It was hard to get a ticket, and probably quite expensive too. My father and Günther bought one ticket which they shared. It was not even a proper seat — just sitting on the stairs between the aisles of the concert hall. My father listened to the first half, went out during the interval, [and Günther took the second half].

    G-F-0042 · p. ~93
    Chapter VII · The Loewens — an itinerary Berlin → Paris → Barcelona → Haifa → Heidelberg · 1933 onwards

    The Loewens had been friends of my parents since the Berlin days. They emigrated to Paris in 1933, from there to Barcelona, and from there to Haifa, where Dr. Loewen worked for the Electrical Company.

    After his retirement, Dr. Loewen decided to attend lectures on chemistry in the American University in Heidelberg, where he died. His widow never moved back to Haifa, and my mother visited her once in Heidelberg.
